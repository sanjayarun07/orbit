# From the web (dated, with sources)
**Provider**: Perplexity web search · **Query**: In Akash Network: apart from staking how else can we tie dual token to the main token?
Resolved from conversation contex

For the Venice-style question—**how to tie a separately tradable “dual token” to AKT without relying on staking**—Akash’s strongest precedent is its **Burn-Mint Equilibrium (BME)** design. But one crucial qualification: **Akash’s second unit, ACT, is expressly non-transferable**, so it is a useful *mechanism template*, not a direct example of a tradable DIEM-like token. [1][5][8]

## What Akash already demonstrates

On **March 23, 2026**, after Proposal 318, Akash activated BME: compute demand causes payment to purchase AKT in the market, burn it, mint the USD-pegged compute credit ACT, and settle usage in ACT; providers can mint ACT back into AKT at the prevailing market price. [1] Akash describes ACT as a **non-transferable** stablecoin/compute credit, generated by burning AKT at the oracle price. [1][5][8]

That produces four non-staking links that Venice could adapt for a *transferable* second token:

1. **Mint-gated-by-main-token demand.**  
   The secondary token is created only when AKT is bought and burned—or, in a less deflationary variation, deposited into a protocol reserve. Thus, secondary-token issuance mechanically creates buy pressure for AKT. [1][8]

2. **Protocol redemption / conversion link.**  
   In Akash, providers receive ACT and can convert it back to AKT at the current market price. [1] A transferable second token could similarly carry an issuer/protocol conversion or redemption pathway into AKT, subject to defined pricing, reserves, and redemption caps.

3. **Usage-linked issuance and retirement.**  
   ACT exists to prepay for compute and is burned at settlement; its issuance is therefore tied to actual cloud consumption rather than emissions or staking rewards. [5] A Venice-like token could be minted only against verifiable network consumption—e.g., inference, data, storage, API calls, or AI-agent spend—and retired when that utility is consumed.

4. **Fiat/stablecoin on-ramp that still routes through AKT.**  
   Akash’s prior USDC-payment design weakened AKT demand, according to Akash; BME changes the flow so a user’s compute purchase automatically buys and burns AKT to produce ACT. [1] This is especially relevant for Venice: users can pay in dollars/stablecoins/card rails, while the protocol forces the economic conversion through the main token.

## Mechanisms worth considering for an AKT-linked, *tradable* second token

### 1. Burn-to-mint tradable service token
Users pay USDC or fiat; the protocol market-buys and burns AKT; it mints an equivalent amount of a transferable token—call it `xAKT-credit`—that can be used for a defined product or marketplace.

**Link to AKT:** every net issuance requires AKT demand and destruction.  
**Why it is stronger than “accept AKT for payment”:** it makes AKT an input to the second token’s supply, rather than merely one optional payment currency. Akash explicitly frames this as making AKT “essential to every transaction.” [8]  
**Main risk:** if the token is redeemable at a fixed dollar value, this creates stablecoin-like liability and reserve/oracle/liquidity risks. Akash avoids some of that market-risk surface by making ACT non-transferable. [1][5]

### 2. Lock-to-mint / reserve-backed issuance
Instead of permanently burning AKT, users or market makers lock AKT into a vault to mint the tradable second token. The protocol can impose overcollateralization, liquidation thresholds, mint caps, and redemption delays.

**Link to AKT:** supply expansion requires AKT collateral; demand for the second token increases demand to acquire/lock AKT.  
**Why use this instead of burning:** it preserves a redeemable backing pool and permits a clearer collateral story.  
**Trade-off:** this resembles collateralized stablecoin design more than Akash BME and can amplify AKT-price reflexivity in drawdowns.

### 3. Fee-funded AKT buyback and second-token distribution
Akash’s earlier design applied fees to stablecoin compute payments; the fees were intended to purchase AKT on a DEX, with some AKT burned and some earmarked for future staker distribution. [9] A dual-token version could instead direct defined protocol revenue to:

- market-buy AKT;
- burn, lock, or treasury-hold that AKT; and
- distribute/mint the tradable second token to users, providers, developers, or liquidity contributors according to product activity.

**Link to AKT:** secondary-token issuance is funded by revenues that create AKT buying pressure.  
**Difference from staking:** recipients earn based on productive behavior—usage, supply, referrals, application development, liquidity, or service provision—not token delegation.

### 4. AKT-denominated issuance auctions
Sell the second token only in auctions payable in AKT, then burn or lock the AKT proceeds. Periodic issuance could be tied to verified growth metrics, such as paid usage or provider supply.

**Link to AKT:** acquiring the second token requires acquiring AKT.  
**Advantage:** simple and transparent; avoids a fixed redemption promise.  
**Weakness:** it links *primary issuance* to AKT but does not inherently support the second token’s ongoing value after issuance.

### 5. AKT-required utility collateral for providers or application operators
Akash’s current BME flow makes providers ultimately receive AKT rather than stablecoin payouts. [5] A transferable second token could instead be the operational medium—e.g., for service credits, priority access, API quota, or marketplace fees—while providers, app operators, or issuers must post AKT collateral to accept or mint it.

**Link to AKT:** productive supply-side participation requires AKT bonding, but the second token can remain liquid and usable by customers.  
**Important distinction:** this is not passive staking yield; it is **performance/security collateral** tied to a commercial role.

### 6. Treasury/risk-token structure
Make the second token a transferable claim on a defined protocol economic stream—such as a share of service-fee revenue, buyback budget, or treasury surplus—while AKT remains the scarce collateral/payment asset that the protocol buys, locks, or burns.

**Link to AKT:** protocol revenue is routed into AKT purchases and reserve management; the second token has a claim on the resulting economic engine rather than being merely inflation-paid.  
**Caution:** a token with revenue rights can introduce substantial securities, tax, and jurisdictional issues; this needs specialized legal analysis before design or launch.

## Best-fit design for Akash specifically

If the objective is to add a **tradable** dual token while preserving the core BME insight, the closest fit is:

> **Fiat/USDC → automatic AKT market buy → AKT burn or time-locked reserve → mint transferable usage/reward token → token is consumed for premium network utility or redeemed under bounded rules.**

That preserves Akash’s key lesson: stablecoin payments under AEP-23 improved usability but weakened native-token demand; BME was introduced specifically to retain stable-dollar UX while restoring a direct AKT-demand path. [1][5][8] Before BME, Akash supported both AKT and USDC settlement, and its 2025 review reported **1,941,782 AKT** versus **$806,726 USDC** in network spend. [2]

For a Venice/DIEM comparison, I would **not** copy ACT’s non-transferability if secondary-market liquidity is a requirement. Instead, copy the **AKT purchase/burn/mint pathway**, then constrain transferability through one or more of:

- issuance only against paid consumption or AKT collateral;
- a hard mint ceiling and utilization-linked emissions;
- protocol buyback/burn funded by real fees;
- capped and delayed redemption;
- clearly bounded utility rather than an open-ended dollar peg.

In short: **the non-staking link is not “hold AKT to earn Token B”; it is “Token B cannot be created, redeemed, or economically sustained without recurring AKT demand, AKT collateral, AKT fee capture, or AKT retirement.”** Akash’s BME is the cleanest current model of the first and fourth versions, even though its ACT is deliberately non-tradable.
